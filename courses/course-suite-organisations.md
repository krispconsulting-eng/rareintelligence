# Rare Disease Capability Courses: Patient Organisations

A suite of courses built from the Subject Repository, scoped to one audience:
the people running or building a rare disease patient organisation. It is the
companion to the families-and-advocates suite. Where that one builds
individuals, this one builds groups: the governance, funding, community, data
and influence that make an organisation strong and keep it going.

Like its companion, every course runs two ways from the same content: as a
self-paced online course with live cohort sessions, and as a session or stream
inside a face-to-face event. The Subject Repository (`subject-repository.md`) is
the source. This document turns the organisation-facing slice of it into
something you can run.

## Who this is for, and who it is not

The repository serves four audiences: organisation leaders (ORG), individual
advocates and consumers (ADV), either (BOTH), and researchers (RES). This suite
keeps the ORG subjects and the BOTH subjects at the depth an organisation
leader needs. It drops the ADV-only material, which the families-and-advocates
suite covers, and the RES-only material.

The learner here is a founder, chair, coordinator, or staff or volunteer lead:
someone with responsibility for an organisation, not just for their own
advocacy. A person preparing to sit on a Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory
Committee (PBAC) process belongs in the advocates suite. A person deciding
whether their group should build a registry belongs here.

## The shape of the suite

Seven courses across four tiers, with a leadership and wellbeing strand that
runs through all of them and also stands alone. The tiers are a growth path: a
new group starts at Foundations, an established one enters at Research readiness
or Influence.

| Tier | Course | Best entry point | Repository domains drawn on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Foundations | 1. Governance, compliance and running an organisation | New or forming groups | A, G (governance) |
| 2. Sustainability | 2. Funding and sustainability | Groups needing to fund the work | G (funding, partnerships) |
| 2. Sustainability | 3. Building and sustaining community | Any group serving a community | G (community), J |
| 3. Research readiness | 4. Data assets and research readiness | Groups near research or industry | B, C, E (CABs), F |
| 3. Research readiness | 5. Making the case to partners and investors | Groups seeking partnership or investment | D, F, G (pitching) |
| 4. Influence | 6. Systemic advocacy and ecosystem building | Groups ready to shape the system | G (ecosystem), H |
| Woven strand | 7. Leading without burning out | Every group, always | I |

No group needs all seven at once. Foundations is enough to stand a compliant
organisation up. The Research readiness tier is only for groups whose community
is near research or industry; a support-focused group can skip it. Course 7 is
not optional: an organisation that burns out its one committed person is not
sustainable, whatever else it does well.

## Tier 1. Foundations

### Course 1. Governance, compliance and running an organisation

**For new or forming groups, and anyone who has never run the compliance side.**

The point of this course is a compliant, well-governed organisation that will
survive its founders. It covers the machinery a group in Australia actually has
to get right, and the landscape it operates in, so a new leader is not learning
governance and the rare disease system at the same time under pressure.

Subjects, drawn from Domains A and G:

- The rare disease landscape, policy, and the National Strategic Action Plan
  for Rare Diseases: the context an organisation operates in.
- Who's who: the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), PBAC and the Medical
  Services Advisory Committee (MSAC), research networks, and what each decides.
- Governance and compliance: running a compliant, well-governed organisation,
  including Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC)
  obligations and a workable constitution.
- The foundations of building and managing a community group, at the level a
  founder needs before the deeper community course.

**Online.** Three self-paced modules on governance and the landscape, plus one
live clinic where a new leader brings their own constitution or compliance
question.

**Face to face.** A half-day foundations morning that opens an organisation
event: a short plenary on the landscape, and a governance workshop where groups
pressure-test their own structure against the compliance obligations.

**Outcome.** A leader can stand up or tidy a compliant, well-governed
organisation, and place it correctly in the Australian rare disease system.

## Tier 2. Sustainability

### Course 2. Funding and sustainability

**For groups that need to fund the work without depending on one grant or one person.**

The point of this course is money that lasts: knowing the funding types, being
ready when a grant opens, running the grant lifecycle, and working with industry
inside the rules. It is where a group moves from surviving year to year toward a
funding base it can plan on.

Subjects, drawn from Domain G:

- Funding, grants, sponsorship and fundraising: the funding types, grant
  readiness, the grant lifecycle, and donor management.
- Industry partnerships and Code compliance: working with industry within the
  Medicines Australia Code of Conduct.
- Strategic planning as it applies to funding: planning the work so the funding
  follows it, not the other way round.

**Online.** Three modules plus a live workshop where a group builds one real
grant application or funding plan with feedback.

**Face to face.** A workshop day: a grant-readiness clinic, and a session on
industry partnerships that sets the Code and a conflict-of-interest approach as
the starting point, not an afterthought.

**Outcome.** A group can fund the work through more than one channel and partner
with industry inside the rules.

### Course 3. Building and sustaining community

**For any group whose job is to serve and grow a community, inclusively.**

The point of this course is a community an organisation can actually reach and
serve, including the people usually left out. It joins community management with
equity and family support, because a group that only reaches the
already-connected is not serving its whole community.

Subjects, drawn from Domains G and J:

- Building and managing community: online groups, social media, moderation, and
  the risks that come with them.
- Reaching priority populations: approaches for First Nations, culturally and
  linguistically diverse, and rural or remote communities.
- Health literacy and accessibility: making information usable for the people
  who need it.
- Codesign with priority populations: designing with, not for, under-served
  communities.
- Sibling, family and carer support: supporting the wider family, including
  siblings of affected children.

**Online.** Three modules plus a live session on moderation and risk using real
scenarios a group brings.

**Face to face.** A workshop day anchored by a codesign exercise with, where
possible, members of the priority populations a group is trying to reach, so the
design is done with them in the room.

**Outcome.** A group can build, moderate and grow a community that reaches
beyond the already-connected, and support the whole family.

## Tier 3. Research readiness

The two Research readiness courses are for groups whose community is near, or
wants to be near, research and industry. A group focused on support and
information can skip this tier without loss.

### Course 4. Data assets and research readiness

**For groups building the data and structures that research and industry need.**

The point of this course is turning a community into one a researcher or company
can actually work with: the data assets, the governance around them, and the
structures that let a community shape research rather than just supply it. This
is the difference between a community that is studied and one that is a partner.

Subjects, drawn from Domains B, C, E and F:

- Registries: working with or setting up a registry, and its governance and use.
- Natural history studies: why they matter, how they are designed, how data is
  collected.
- Patient-generated data and data sharing: what a community can contribute, and
  the terms on which it shares it.
- Trial readiness for organisations: moving a community from awareness to
  genuine readiness through endpoints, a team, and momentum.
- Community Advisory Boards: standing patient bodies that inform trial design
  and endpoints.
- Data strategy and assets that de-risk development: designing datasets that
  attract industry and satisfy regulators.
- Data governance, intellectual property (IP) ownership and consent: who owns
  and controls the data a community builds.
- Publication strategy: when and how to publish, and why it matters for
  partnership.
- Digital health tools and artificial intelligence (AI): where they sit in a
  community's data and research work, with the benefits and the risks named.

**Online.** Four modules with worked examples, plus a live "design your data
asset" workshop where a group sketches the registry or dataset it needs.

**Face to face.** A workshop day built around two pieces: a data-strategy
breakout where groups design an asset that de-risks development, and a
governance and IP session that settles who owns and controls the data before it
is built.

**Outcome.** A group can design and govern the data assets and structures that
make its community a credible research and industry partner.

### Course 5. Making the case to partners and investors

**For groups ready to seek partnership or investment.**

The point of this course is the pitch: making the case for partnership and
investment to biopharma and investors, backed by the evidence that access
decisions actually turn on. It forces investment-readiness thinking, which
sharpens everything upstream of it.

Subjects, drawn from Domains D, F and G:

- Investment readiness and pitching: making the case for partnership and
  investment to biopharma and investors.
- Market access, reimbursement and payers: what determines whether an approved
  therapy actually reaches patients, from the organisation's angle.
- Real-world data and evidence for access: generating evidence that supports
  access decisions.
- Publication strategy, as it supports the case for partnership.

**Online.** Three modules plus a live pitch rehearsal with feedback.

**Face to face.** A pitch session with expert judges as the finale: groups
present their case for partnership or investment and are pressure-tested by
people who sit on the funding side. This is the RDDS-style capstone, and it
concentrates the mind more than any workshop.

**Outcome.** A group can make a credible, evidence-backed case for partnership
or investment and stand up to the questions that follow.

## Tier 4. Influence

### Course 6. Systemic advocacy and ecosystem building

**For groups ready to shape the system, and to share the load across the sector.**

The point of this course is leverage at the organisational level: moving from
one group's cause to systemic change, and coordinating beyond a single disease
so the whole sector carries more. It uses the National Strategic Action Plan for
Rare Diseases as a working tool.

Subjects, drawn from Domains H and G:

- Systemic advocacy and campaigns: moving from individual to systemic change.
- Government submissions and policy engagement: planning, writing and lodging a
  submission that lands.
- Working with the National Strategic Action Plan for Rare Diseases as an
  advocacy tool.
- International cooperation and networks: building and using cross-border
  alliances.
- Strategic planning and ecosystem building: coordinating beyond one disease and
  sharing the load across organisations.

**Online.** Three modules plus a live submission clinic where groups draft and
critique a real submission together.

**Face to face.** A masterclass day: a campaign-planning workshop, a submission
lab, and a community-of-practice session where groups agree how to share the
load across the sector rather than each carrying it alone.

**Outcome.** A group can run a systemic campaign, lodge an effective submission,
and coordinate with others so capability spreads across the sector.

## Woven strand

### Course 7. Leading without burning out

**For every group, at every tier.**

This is the strand that runs through the whole suite and also stands alone. It
is here because rare disease organisations are too often one exhausted person,
and an organisation that depends on one person is fragile. Leading without
burning out is an organisational capability, not a personal indulgence.

Subjects, drawn from Domain I:

- Leadership in advocacy: shared and distributed models that do not rest on a
  single person or group.
- Mentoring, peer learning and communities of practice: the structures that make
  learning compound after an event.
- Resilience and wellbeing for staff and volunteers.
- Mental health in rare disease, as a companion to formal Mental Health First
  Aid (MHFA).
- Self-care and boundaries: holding the line between personal and professional.
- Storytelling and lived-experience sharing, handled safely, as an
  organisational skill.

**Online.** A short standalone module set, plus a leadership and wellbeing check
woven into the close of every other course.

**Face to face.** A facilitated session in every event on distributing
leadership and building the mentoring and community structures that keep a group
going.

**Outcome.** A group can spread leadership, sustain its people, and keep the
learning compounding after any single leader moves on.

## How the suite is delivered

The same seven courses run through two delivery models, both blended so live
time is spent on application rather than basics.

### The online academy

- **Blended, not just recorded.** Each course is self-paced modules plus live
  cohort sessions where the application happens: clinics, workshops, pitch
  rehearsals, submission labs.
- **Tiered commitment.** Each course offers three depths so groups enter where
  they can manage: a short introduction of roughly two hours, a fast-track of
  about a day's worth, and the full course. A stretched volunteer still gets
  something real; a funded coordinator gets the depth they need.
- **A foundation tier that is accessible.** Course 1 and Course 7 should be the
  most reachable, because governance and sustainable leadership are where new
  groups most often come unstuck.
- **It compounds.** A community of practice, an alumni network, and small-group
  mentoring turn a finished course into a capability the sector keeps. For
  organisations this matters doubly: the point is to spread capability across
  groups, not to concentrate it in a few.

### The face-to-face event

The same content assembles into a live program. A two-day organisation
intensive maps cleanly onto the suite; the sample agenda is in
`event-blueprint-org-two-day.md`. The design features worth holding to:

- **Mandatory pre-work.** The Course 1 online modules are completed before
  anyone arrives, so the room is spent on application.
- **Experiential over lecture.** The clinics, the data-strategy breakout, the
  pitch session and the submission lab are where capability is built.
- **One place per organisation or region where capacity is tight.** Spread
  capability across groups rather than concentrating it in a few.
- **A community-of-practice launch.** End with the structure that keeps groups
  learning from each other after the room empties.
- **Take-home toolkits.** Send every group away with a resource library, so the
  reach extends past those who could attend.

## Australian anchors

The repository names European and United States bodies because that is where its
source programs ran. This suite uses the Australian equivalents throughout:

| Function | Use in this suite |
|---|---|
| Charity regulation and governance | Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC) |
| Medicines regulation | Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) |
| Reimbursement and HTA | PBAC and MSAC |
| National policy | National Strategic Action Plan for Rare Diseases |
| Research involvement | NHMRC Statement on Consumer and Community Involvement |
| Industry-facing content | Medicines Australia Code of Conduct |

Any industry-supported delivery builds in Code of Conduct compliance and a clear
conflict-of-interest approach from the design stage. Public-facing and
pharma-adjacent material names no product and implies no endorsement.

## A note on depth

Depth beats coverage here too. A group that does governance, funding and
sustainable leadership well is worth more than one that has sat through all seven
courses and embedded none. The tiers exist so a group takes what its stage
actually needs: Foundations to stand up, Sustainability to keep going, Research
readiness only when its community is near research, Influence when it is ready to
shape the system, and the leadership strand always. Run fewer things properly,
and let the community of practice carry the rest across the sector.

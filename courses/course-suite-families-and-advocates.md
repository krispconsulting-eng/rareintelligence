# Rare Disease Capability Courses: Families and Advocates

A suite of courses built from the Subject Repository, scoped to one audience:
families living with a rare disease, and the advocates who speak for their
communities. Every course is designed to run two ways from the same content:
as a self-paced online course with live cohort sessions, and as a session or
stream inside a face-to-face event. Pick the delivery that fits the moment;
the learning is the same.

This is the built version of the menu. The Subject Repository
(`subject-repository.md`) is the source you select and re-source from. This
document turns a slice of it into something you can actually run.

## Who this is for, and who it is not

The repository serves four audiences: organisation leaders (ORG), individual
advocates and consumers (ADV), either (BOTH), and researchers (RES). This
suite deliberately drops the ORG-only and RES-only subjects. It keeps the
BOTH and ADV material, plus the family and carer subjects from Domain J, and
pitches all of it at two levels of one audience:

- **Families.** People new to a rare disease diagnosis, or living alongside
  one, who need to understand the system they have landed in and stay well
  while they do.
- **Advocates.** People ready to act as research, regulatory or health
  technology assessment (HTA) partners, and to move from individual concern
  to systemic change.

A family finding their feet and an advocate preparing to sit on a Pharmaceutical
Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) process need different depths of the same
subjects. The tiers below keep the level right.

## The shape of the suite

Six courses across three tiers, with a wellbeing strand that runs through all
of them and also stands alone. The tiers are a pathway: a family can enter at
Orientation and climb, or an experienced advocate can start at Partnering and
skip what they already know.

| Tier | Course | Primary learner | Repository domains drawn on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Orientation | 1. Understanding the rare disease system | Families | A, B (diagnosis, genetics) |
| 2. Partnering | 2. From lived experience to evidence | Advocates | E, I (storytelling), B (patient data) |
| 2. Partnering | 3. Inside research and clinical trials | Advocates | B, C |
| 2. Partnering | 4. Access, regulation and HTA in Australia | Advocates | D |
| 3. Influence | 5. Advocacy that moves the system | Advocates | H |
| Woven strand | 6. Sustaining yourself in this work | Both | I, J (sibling, carer, family) |

The pathway is not a staircase everyone must climb. Course 1 is enough on its
own for a family that only wants to understand where they are. Courses 2 to 5
are the advocacy ladder. Course 6 is not optional decoration: the work is
emotionally demanding, so wellbeing is built in, not bolted on.

## Tier 1. Orientation

### Course 1. Understanding the rare disease system

**For families, newly affected or newly engaged. No prior knowledge assumed.**

The point of this course is orientation: a family finishes it able to map the
system they are now part of, name the people who make decisions in it, and
understand their own diagnostic pathway. It is the level-setter the whole
suite rests on.

Subjects, drawn from Domains A and B:

- The rare disease landscape, policy, and the National Strategic Action Plan
  for Rare Diseases.
- The therapy lifecycle, from gene discovery to access, told once as a whole
  arc so nothing later feels like a foreign country.
- Who's who: the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), PBAC and the Medical
  Services Advisory Committee (MSAC), research networks, and what each decides.
- Scientific and regulatory literacy foundations, enough shared vocabulary to
  make every later course readable.
- Genetics and genomics fundamentals.
- Diagnosis and the diagnostic odyssey: pathways, genetic counselling,
  secondary and additional findings, newborn screening.

**Online.** Four self-paced modules with short videos and a glossary, plus one
live welcome webinar and one live question session. A family can complete it in
their own time across a fortnight.

**Face to face.** A half-day orientation morning that opens an event: a short
plenary on the landscape, a walk through the therapy lifecycle, and a
facilitated "map your own pathway" exercise using each family's real
diagnostic story. This is the mandatory pre-work made social.

**Outcome.** A family can describe the system, name the actors, and place their
own experience inside the arc from discovery to access.

## Tier 2. Partnering

The three Partnering courses build an advocate who can sit credibly at a
research, regulatory or reimbursement table. They can be taken in any order,
though the sequence below is the one that compounds best.

### Course 2. From lived experience to evidence

**For advocates ready to work as partners, not subjects.**

Lived experience is the thing an advocate brings that no one else can. This
course is about turning it into evidence a researcher, regulator or committee
can actually use, and about telling the story safely and to effect. It is the
hinge between having an experience and putting it to work.

Subjects, drawn from Domain E, plus storytelling from I and patient data from B:

- Principles and models of engagement across the lifecycle: where and how
  patients add value, from discovery to access.
- Being a research partner and co-designing as an equal, not a token.
- Patient experts: the roles, the capabilities, and how to manage independence
  and conflict of interest.
- Patient journeys and lived-experience evidence: turning experience into
  something usable.
- Patient-generated data and data sharing: what you can contribute, and the
  terms on which you share it.
- Storytelling and lived-experience sharing: telling it well, safely, and to
  effect.

**Online.** Three modules plus a facilitated live workshop where each advocate
shapes one piece of their own experience into a usable contribution and gets
peer and facilitator feedback.

**Face to face.** A half-day workshop built around a co-design exercise and a
facilitated storytelling session. The storytelling session is run with care:
safety and boundaries first, effect second.

**Outcome.** An advocate can contribute lived experience as evidence, work as a
genuine partner, and tell their story without being consumed by it.

### Course 3. Inside research and clinical trials

**For advocates who want to read and question the science, not just be briefed on it.**

This course gives an advocate enough science literacy to sit at a research
table and hold their own: how a therapy is developed, how a trial is designed,
what is measured, and where the ethics live. It is not a science degree. It is
the literacy that lets a partner ask the right question.

Subjects, drawn from Domains B and C:

- Translational research, natural history studies, and where advocates fit.
- Gene, advanced and genome-editing therapies, and the ethics of new modalities.
- Research and genomic data ethics: consent, governance, and the real tensions.
- The drug and therapeutic development lifecycle, end to end.
- Clinical trial design and methodology, including small-population designs,
  N-of-1 and N-of-few, and decentralised trials.
- Outcome measures, endpoints and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), and how
  patient priorities shape them.
- Fundamentals of statistics, enough to read and question a result.
- Ethics in trials and what an ethics committee actually does, experienced from
  the inside.
- Pharmacovigilance, safety and benefit-risk after approval.

**Online.** Four modules with worked examples on real disease cases, plus a
live "read a trial together" session where the cohort appraises one published
study line by line.

**Face to face.** A workshop day anchored by two experiential pieces: a
hands-on endpoints exercise where advocates argue for the outcomes that matter
to their community, and an ethics-committee role-play where they review a
protocol from the inside.

**Outcome.** An advocate can read a trial, question an endpoint, and partner
credibly across the research lifecycle.

### Course 4. Access, regulation and HTA in Australia

**For advocates preparing to contribute to, or sit on, an access decision.**

Approval is not access. This course is about the machinery that decides whether
an approved therapy actually reaches patients in Australia, and how an advocate
takes part in it. It is anchored to the Australian system throughout: the TGA
for regulation, PBAC and MSAC for reimbursement and HTA.

Subjects, drawn from Domain D:

- Regulatory pathways and engagement: orphan designation, scientific advice,
  and how to engage the TGA.
- Compassionate use and early or expanded access: getting a therapy before or
  outside standard approval.
- HTA fundamentals: what it is, how it decides, and the methods underneath.
- Patient involvement in HTA: contributing as a patient expert to PBAC and
  MSAC processes.
- Critical appraisal of HTA reports: reading, questioning and responding to an
  assessment.
- Market access, reimbursement and payers: what determines whether a therapy
  reaches patients.
- Real-world data and evidence for access.
- Health economics basics: the cost-effectiveness logic advocates meet in HTA.

**Online.** Four modules plus expert office hours, twenty-minute one-to-one
slots where an advocate takes their real submission or question to someone who
has sat on the other side of the table.

**Face to face.** A workshop day built around a mini PBAC or MSAC simulation:
advocates prepare and present a consumer input, then see how a committee weighs
it. Critical appraisal is practised on a real, recent assessment.

**Outcome.** An advocate can contribute a consumer input to a PBAC or MSAC
process and appraise the evidence behind a decision.

## Tier 3. Influence

### Course 5. Advocacy that moves the system

**For advocates moving from individual concern to systemic change.**

The final tier is about leverage: turning one family's problem into a change in
the system, through campaigns, submissions, and the national framework. It uses
the National Strategic Action Plan for Rare Diseases as a working tool, not a
document to admire.

Subjects, drawn from Domain H:

- Systemic advocacy and campaigns: moving from individual to systemic change.
- Government submissions and policy engagement: planning, writing and lodging
  one that lands.
- Working with the National Strategic Action Plan for Rare Diseases as an
  advocacy tool.
- International cooperation and networks: building and using cross-border
  alliances.
- Engaging the legislative and institutional system: how decisions are made,
  and where to intervene.

**Online.** Three modules plus a live submission clinic where advocates draft
and critique a real submission together.

**Face to face.** A masterclass day: a campaign-planning workshop, a submission
lab, and a panel that shows how a decision actually gets made and where an
advocate can intervene.

**Outcome.** An advocate can plan a campaign, write and lodge an effective
submission, and use the national framework as a lever.

## Woven strand

### Course 6. Sustaining yourself in this work

**For everyone, families and advocates, at every tier.**

This is the strand that runs through the whole suite and also stands alone. It
is here because the work is emotionally demanding and the people who do it burn
out. Treating wellbeing as core content, not a closing thank-you, is a
deliberate design choice borrowed from the programs that do this best.

Subjects, drawn from Domains I and J:

- Resilience and wellbeing for people doing emotionally demanding work.
- Mental health in rare disease, as a companion to formal Mental Health First
  Aid (MHFA).
- Self-care and boundaries: holding the line between personal and professional.
- Storytelling and lived-experience sharing, handled safely.
- Sibling, family and carer support, including siblings of affected children.
- Health literacy and accessibility, so the material reaches the people who
  need it.

**Online.** A short standalone module set, plus a wellbeing check woven into
the close of every other course so it is never separate from the substance.

**Face to face.** A facilitated wellbeing session in every event, and a family
and carer support stream that supports the whole family, not only the person at
the table.

**Outcome.** People can sustain the work, and families are supported as a whole.

## How the suite is delivered

The same six courses run through two delivery models. Both use a blended design
so that live time, online or in a room, is spent on application rather than
basics.

### The online academy

- **Blended, not just recorded.** Each course is self-paced modules plus live
  cohort sessions. The live sessions are where application happens: workshops,
  clinics, office hours, appraisal.
- **Tiered commitment.** Each course offers three depths so people enter where
  they can manage: a short introduction of roughly two hours, a fast-track of
  about a day's worth, and the full course. A family with no time still gets
  something real; an advocate with a submission due gets the depth they need.
- **A foundation tier that is free or low cost.** Course 1 and Course 6 should
  be the most accessible, because they reach the people who will never be in a
  room. Free, plainly written, and available in the formats priority
  populations actually use.
- **It compounds.** A community of practice, an alumni network, and small-group
  mentoring turn a finished course into a capability that keeps growing. This is
  the difference between a course people take and a capability a community
  keeps.

### The face-to-face event

The same content assembles into a live program. A flagship two-day event maps
cleanly onto the suite:

- **Day one, orientation and partnering.** Course 1 as the opening morning,
  then breakouts drawn from Courses 2 and 3. The ethics-committee role-play and
  the endpoints exercise are the anchors.
- **Day two, access and influence.** A PBAC or MSAC simulation from Course 4,
  expert office hours, and a submission lab from Course 5. The wellbeing session
  and family stream from Course 6 run across both days.

Design features worth holding to, all drawn from programs that have proven
them:

- **Mandatory pre-work.** The Course 1 online modules are completed before
  anyone arrives, so the room is spent on application, not definitions.
- **Experiential over lecture.** The strongest sessions are the simulations,
  clinics and office hours, not the slides. Weight the agenda accordingly.
- **A funded carer place.** Cover accommodation for a registered carer where
  needed. Real access, not just a fee waiver.
- **One place per family or region where capacity is tight.** Spread capability
  rather than concentrate it, so more communities carry it home.
- **Take-home toolkits.** Send every participant away with a resource library,
  so the reach extends past the people who could attend.

## Reaching everyone the suite is meant to reach

Domain J of the repository is largely written for organisations, but its intent
belongs in the delivery design of any course aimed at families. Two things
follow:

- **Priority populations by design.** First Nations, culturally and
  linguistically diverse, and rural or remote families are reached only if the
  suite is designed for them from the start: plain language, the right formats,
  and codesign with those communities rather than delivery at them.
- **Accessibility is not a setting.** Health literacy and accessibility shape
  every module. Material a family cannot use is material that does not exist.

## Australian anchors

The repository names European and United States bodies because that is where
its source programs ran. For an Australian audience, every one of them has a
local equivalent, and this suite uses the local one throughout:

| Function | Use in this suite |
|---|---|
| Medicines regulation | Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) |
| Reimbursement and HTA | PBAC and MSAC |
| National policy | National Strategic Action Plan for Rare Diseases |
| Research involvement | NHMRC Statement on Consumer and Community Involvement |
| Industry-facing content | Medicines Australia Code of Conduct |

Any industry-supported delivery builds in Code of Conduct compliance and a
clear conflict-of-interest approach from the design stage. Public-facing and
pharma-adjacent material names no product and implies no endorsement.

---

## A note on depth

Depth beats coverage. A suite that does six courses well is worth more than one
that names twenty subjects and lands none. The audience tags exist to stop
organisational content being pitched at a family finding their feet, or
foundation content being pitched at an advocate preparing for a committee. Keep
the level right, run fewer things properly, and let the community of practice
carry the rest.
